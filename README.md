<h1><strong>C Corp vs. S Corp: A Complete Guide to the Key Differences</strong></h1>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A C corporation is a separate taxpayer that pays federal income tax on its profits, and its shareholders pay tax again on dividends.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An S corporation passes its income, losses, deductions, and credits through to its shareholders, who report them on personal returns, so the company itself usually owes no federal income tax.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In exchange, an S corporation has to follow strict eligibility rules that a C corporation doesn't.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">One point trips up many first-time founders. "S corp" isn't a different type of company. It's a tax status that an eligible corporation (or an LLC) can elect with the IRS.&nbsp;</span></p>

<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This guide explains how each structure is taxed, who can own each one, how liability, payroll taxes, and fundraising differ, and which fits different kinds of businesses.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Facts below carry a source and a date. Tax rules change, so confirm your situation with a CPA or attorney before you file.</span></p>
<h2><strong>The Short Answer</strong></h2>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>C corp:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Taxed as its own entity, at a flat 21% federal rate under the tax code, with shareholders taxed again on dividends.&nbsp;</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Best for companies that plan to raise venture capital, issue multiple classes of stock, or sell or go public someday.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>S corp:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Pass-through taxation with no corporate-level federal income tax in most cases.&nbsp;</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Limited to 100 shareholders and one class of stock, with no corporate, partnership, or nonresident alien owners. Best for small, closely held, profitable businesses.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Quick rule:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Seeking outside investors? Start with a C corporation. Running a profitable small business with a few U.S. owners? Look at an S election.</span></li>
</ul>

<h2><strong>How a C Corporation Works</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A C corporation is the default form of a corporation under tax law. It files its own return and pays its own tax.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">According to the IRS, a C corporation is a separate taxing entity, and its income "is taxed to the corporation when earned, and then is taxed to the shareholders when distributed as dividends."&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The corporation gets no deduction for the dividends it pays, and shareholders can't deduct the corporation's losses (IRS, corporations page, reviewed June 17, 2026).</span></p>
<h3><strong>The tax rate</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The federal corporate income tax rate is a flat 21% of taxable income under 26 U.S.C. 11(b). Your state may charge its own corporate tax on top.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Why C corps are common for startups</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>No limit on shareholders.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> You can add as many investors as you need.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Multiple stock classes.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Preferred stock, common stock, and other classes let you shape investor deals.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Any type of investor.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Funds, other corporations, and foreign investors can hold shares.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>QSBS potential.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Founders and investors in a qualifying C corporation can exclude part or all of their gain on a sale, as explained below.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The SBA says a C corporation suits companies that need to raise money through stock sales and those planning to go public or be sold (SBA, page modified August 28, 2026).&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It also warns that corporate profits are taxed twice.</span></p>
<h2><strong>How an S Corporation Works</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An S corporation is a corporation that elects a special tax status. Income, losses, deductions, and credits flow through to shareholders, who report them on their personal returns and pay tax at individual rates.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That avoids the double tax on corporate income, according to the IRS (S corporations page, last reviewed June 11, 2026).</span></p>
<h3><strong>What forms are involved</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The company files Form 1120-S, the U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Each shareholder receives a Schedule K-1 and reports the amounts on Form 1040 and Schedule E.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Shareholders may need to make estimated payments with Form 1040-ES.</span></li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Who qualifies</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The IRS lists these requirements:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The business must be a domestic corporation.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Allowed shareholders are individuals, certain trusts, and estates. Partnerships, corporations, and nonresident alien shareholders aren't allowed.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The company can have no more than 100 shareholders.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">It can have only one class of stock.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">It can't be an ineligible corporation, such as certain financial institutions or insurance companies.</span></li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>How to elect</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">File Form 2553, Election by a Small Business Corporation, which must be signed by all the shareholders.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The IRS says the form is used by qualifying small business corporations and limited liability companies.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The IRS's Form 2553 page doesn't state the filing deadline, so read the form instructions or ask a tax professional, since missing it can delay your status.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Watch out for entity-level taxes and state rules</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The IRS notes that an S corporation may still owe entity-level tax on certain built-in gains and passive income.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The SBA adds that state treatment varies, and some states tax S corporations differently or don't recognize the election at all.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Payroll Taxes and "Reasonable Compensation"</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Many owners are drawn to S corporations because of how they pay themselves.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An S corporation shareholder who works in the business is an employee, and the IRS says the company must pay that shareholder-employee reasonable compensation before making non-wage distributions (IRS, S corporation compensation page, reviewed March 3, 2026).</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages, which would make them subject to employment taxes.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It looks at factors such as the shareholder's training and experience, duties, time devoted, what comparable businesses pay, and the company's pay history.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In short, you can't pay yourself a tiny salary and take everything else as distributions.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">By contrast, the SBA says LLC members are considered self-employed and owe self-employment tax toward Medicare and Social Security.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An S election can change how that tax applies, but only a CPA can tell you whether it saves money in your case.</span></p>
<h2><strong>The Qualified Business Income Deduction</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Pass-through owners may qualify for the qualified business income (QBI) deduction, which is up to 20% of QBI.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The IRS says S corporation shareholders may qualify, but income from a C corporation isn't eligible, and reasonable compensation paid by an S corporation is excluded from QBI (IRS, QBI deduction page, updated September 22, 2026).</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The same page describes changes for tax years beginning after 2025. For those years, the deduction is the greater of the taxable income limitation or a Minimum Deduction for Active QBI, and the business must be an active qualified trade or business in which the taxpayer materially participates.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The deduction also faces limits tied to taxable income, the type of business, wages paid, and qualified property. Because the rules are detailed, ask a tax professional how they apply to you.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Raising Money: Why Investors Prefer C Corps</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">S corporation rules make venture funding hard. Venture funds are often organized as partnerships, and the IRS says partnerships can't be S corporation shareholders.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">S corporations can issue only one class of stock, which rules out the preferred stock many investors expect.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That's why startups chasing outside funding usually form as Delaware C corporations.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Qualified small business stock (QSBS)</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Section 1202 of the tax code lets certain shareholders exclude part of their gain from selling qualified small business stock.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The statute defines this stock as stock in a domestic C corporation, which means S corporation shares don't qualify (26 U.S.C. 1202).</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The One Big Beautiful Bill Act changed the rules for stock issued after July 4, 2025. According to Perkins Coie's summary:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Tiered exclusion:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> 50% after more than three years, 75% after four years, and 100% after five years</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Higher cap:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> $15 million per issuer, up from $10 million, with inflation adjustments starting in 2027</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Higher asset ceiling:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> $75 million in gross assets, up from $50 million</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 keeps the earlier rules, including the 100% exclusion after five years and the $10 million cap.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">QSBS has additional tests, so review it with a tax attorney before you count on it.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Liability Protection</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Both structures protect owners from personal liability in most cases. The SBA's comparison table says owners of C corporations, S corporations, and LLCs are not personally liable, and it describes a corporation as offering the strongest protection to its owners.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That protection depends on keeping the company separate: use a business bank account, keep records, and follow state filing rules.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Can an LLC Choose Either One?</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yes. The IRS says an LLC is a business structure allowed by state statute, and that it's taxed as a disregarded entity, a partnership, or a corporation depending on its elections and number of members.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">An LLC can file Form 8832 to be taxed as a corporation, and the IRS says Form 2553 is available to qualifying small business corporations and limited liability companies seeking S status (IRS, LLC page, reviewed October 10, 2026, and Form 2553 page, reviewed March 30, 2026).&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Plenty of small businesses form an LLC first and add an S election later if it makes sense.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Which One Should You Choose?</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>Choose a C corporation if you:</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Plan to raise venture capital or sell shares to many investors</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Want to issue preferred stock or multiple classes of shares</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Expect foreign or entity investors</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Are aiming for an acquisition or public offering</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Want your shares to qualify for QSBS treatment</span></li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Choose an S corporation if you:</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Own a profitable small business with a few U.S.-based owners</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Don't need outside equity investors</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Want pass-through taxation and the possibility of the QBI deduction</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Are comfortable running payroll and paying yourself a reasonable salary</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Operate in a state that recognizes the S election</span></li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Talk to a CPA or attorney first if you:</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Have partners with different contributions or exit plans</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Operate in several states</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Expect to sell the company in the next few years</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Own appreciated assets that you plan to move into the company</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>How to Form a Corporation</strong></h2>
<ol>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Pick a state and check name availability.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Most owners form in the state where they operate. Delaware is a common choice for startups seeking investors.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Appoint a registered agent.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> The SBA says a business needs one in the state where it registers.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>File your formation document.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> For a corporation, that is typically the certificate or articles of incorporation, filed with the state business office.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Adopt bylaws and issue founder stock.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Record your shareholders and shares.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Get an EIN.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> The IRS says the online tool issues an EIN "for free," so don't pay a site that charges for one.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Elect S status if it fits.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> File Form 2553 with all shareholders' signatures.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Open a business bank account</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and keep personal funds separate.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Calendar annual filings.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Missed reports can lead to penalties or loss of good standing.</span></li>
</ol>

<h2><strong>Frequently Asked Questions</strong></h2>
<h3><strong>Is an S corp better than a C corp?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Neither is better for everyone. An S corporation avoids double taxation and suits small, closely held businesses. A C corporation suits companies that need multiple stock classes, outside investors, and long-term growth.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Can I be both a C corp and an S corp?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Not at the same time. A corporation is either taxed as a C corporation or elects S status, though it can change status under IRS rules, and a change can have tax consequences.</span></p>
<h3><strong>What is the C corporation tax rate?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The federal rate is 21% of taxable income under 26 U.S.C. 11(b). Shareholders also pay tax on dividends, and states may add their own corporate taxes.</span></p>
<h3><strong>How many shareholders can an S corp have?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">No more than 100, and they must be individuals, certain trusts, or estates. Partnerships, corporations, and nonresident aliens can't be shareholders.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Do S corp owners avoid self-employment tax?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Not entirely. S corporation shareholder-employees must receive reasonable compensation, which is subject to employment taxes, and the IRS can reclassify distributions as wages.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Can an LLC elect to be taxed as an S corp?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yes. The IRS says Form 2553 is available to qualifying small business corporations and LLCs, and an LLC can also elect corporate treatment through Form 8832.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Which is better for a startup?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you plan to raise venture capital, a C corporation is the usual choice, partly because S corporations can't have preferred stock or partnership investors. A bootstrapped, profitable startup may do better with an LLC or an S election.</span></p>
<h3><strong>Does an S corp get the QBI deduction?</strong></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It can. The IRS says S corporation shareholders may qualify, but wages and reasonable compensation are excluded, and income from a C corporation isn't eligible.</span></p>
<h2><strong>The Bottom Line</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Choose a C corporation when growth, outside investors, and a future sale drive your plans.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Choose an S election when you run a profitable, closely held business and want pass-through taxes with a reasonable salary.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Since S status is a tax election, many owners start as an LLC and elect later, rather than locking in too early.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Check your eligibility, price the taxes with a CPA, and confirm any formation service's pricing and inclusions before you pay.</span></p>
